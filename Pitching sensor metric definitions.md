# Pin down WYNG's five undefined pitching metrics

Published research gives WYNG enough to fix four of its five open metric definitions now and to bound the fifth. Arm slot should be the elevation of the forearm long axis above horizontal at ball release, which matches the Motus/Driveline PULSE convention that WYNG's users already know. Arm load should be peak net internal elbow varus torque, computed by two-segment Newton–Euler inverse dynamics from the upper-arm and forearm pucks, with WYNG choosing and labelling one scale. Its own numbers will not match motion capture or PULSE without a validation study, and no wearable has yet shown good absolute agreement. Ball release can be taken from the forearm acceleration peak and maximum external rotation (MER) from upper-arm rotation velocity. Foot plant cannot be detected from the arm in any published method, so it should be shown as an estimate about 150 ms before release, with a ±40 ms uncertainty. The recovery side of the Daily CBR score fits a Banister-style decaying fatigue state with a 1–1.5 day time constant, anchored to the MLB Pitch Smart rest tables. Optional inputs should be modifiers that equal exactly 1 when missing. The pitch classifier needs three corrections that matter more than any tuning: shrink and partially pool the covariances, use Gaussian log-likelihoods instead of softmax of −D, and replace χ² cutoffs with small-sample F cutoffs. At n = 10, χ² cutoffs wrongly reject a third of genuine throws. The biggest hardware risk overlays all of this: forearm and upper-arm angular rates likely exceed ±4000 dps, and forearm accelerations reach about 130 g. Saturation therefore lands exactly at the release instant that arm slot, arm load and event timing all depend on.

|---|---|---|---|
| Arm slot | Forearm elevation above horizontal at release, `asin(forearm_axis · up)`; provisional labels | High on definition, medium on cutoffs | Category cutoffs; validation against video or mocap; behaviour under gyro clipping |

| Classifier | z-score, OAS shrinkage, partial pooling, Gaussian log-likelihood softmax, F-based or empirical cutoffs, percentile Consistency | High on structure, medium on exact parameters | Leave-one-session-out tuning; temperature; final Consistency form |

## Arm slot means forearm elevation at release, not the lab's shoulder-to-hand angle

Two incompatible arm-slot definitions dominate, and WYNG can reproduce only one of them. Consumer forearm sleeves define arm slot as **the angle between the forearm and the ground at ball release**: 0° is sidearm, about 45° is three-quarter, and negative values are submarine ([Driveline](https://drivelinebaseball.com/blogs/blog/motus-throw-what-do-these-numbers-mean); [PULSE Throw wiki](https://s3.amazonaws.com/helpscout.net/docs/assets/560953b490336042cb1d743d/attachments/61abb2ea48287b4578809007/PULSE_Throw_8.8_wiki.pdf)). Lab biomechanics measures something else. Manzi et al. define arm slot as the angle between vertical and the shoulder-to-hand vector, viewed from home plate at release. They report **50° ± 11° for high-school and 58° ± 14° for professional pitchers**, where smaller means more overhand ([Manzi et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10601404/)). Escamilla et al. grouped 207 professionals using the same from-vertical convention: overhand ≤40°, three-quarter 50–60°, sidearm ≥70° ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324507653_Differences_Among_Overhand_Three-Quarter_and_Sidearm_Pitching_Biomechanics_in_Professional_Baseball_Players)). A 927-pitcher MLB cohort used a Statcast-style horizontal reference instead, with sidearm below 33.5° and overhand above 44.4°. Its authors call the arm-slot literature "relatively sparse and highly heterogeneous" ([PMC13519161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13519161/)).

**Proposal.** Define arm slot as the elevation of the forearm long axis (elbow toward wrist) above the plane perpendicular to gravity, at the release sample, in degrees, positive above horizontal. To compute it, first calibrate the sensor-to-segment alignment with a static pose: arm hanging, then forearm horizontal. Then run orientation fusion on the forearm puck, re-anchoring gravity in the quiet pre-throw part of the 2 s window. During the delivery, gate out the accelerometer correction, because |a| is far above 1 g, and rely on gyro integration through the roughly 150 ms delivery. At the release index, compute `arm_slot = asin(forearm_axis_world · up)`. Yaw is unobservable without a magnetometer, but it does not enter this formula, so the missing magnetometer costs nothing here. Because the app already stores upper-arm and forearm lengths, WYNG can also build a shoulder-to-hand proxy from both segment orientations and report it as a secondary "lab-style" value. Do not convert between conventions with a simple 90° − θ rule: elbow flexion at release makes the forearm line and the shoulder-to-hand line differ.

**Evidence on accuracy.** Two studies compared the Motus forearm sleeve against marker motion capture. The Driveline-co-authored study found arm slot **strongly correlated (R = 0.975) but about 7–10° low** ([Boddy et al. 2019](https://peerj.com/articles/6365/)). The independent Mayo Clinic study found a 5.0° mean difference. It judged the device not valid for absolute arm slot, but its arm slot showed good reliability, with under 10° of error ([Camp et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/03635465211029017)). Driveline warns that sensor rotation inside the sleeve is the main error source. It also notes that lateral trunk tilt changes a gravity-referenced arm slot, because the sensor sees forearm-versus-ground and not forearm-versus-torso ([Driveline placement guide](https://drivelinebaseball.com/2017/08/use-motus-sleeve)). WYNG should expect **±5–10° absolute error and a few degrees of within-pitcher repeatability**. That is good enough for trends and categories, but not for fine differences between pitchers.

**Confidence and open items.** Confidence is high on the definition and medium on accuracy. No published scheme validates category cutoffs for the forearm-versus-horizontal convention. The research notes infer provisional labels from the Driveline anchors: over-the-top above about 60°, three-quarter about 30–60°, sidearm about −10° to 30°, and submarine below about −10°. The app should mark these as provisional until WYNG's own distribution is known. Arm slot computed through a span where the gyro has clipped inherits the integration error (see the saturation section). WYNG needs a validation set of at least 20 pitchers filmed with video, plus a rule for when arm slot is withheld.

## Arm load should be net elbow varus torque on a declared scale

"Arm load", as used in the literature and by Motus, is the **peak net internal elbow varus moment**. This is the moment the upper arm exerts on the forearm to resist the valgus moment produced by the inertia of the forearm, hand and ball. It peaks **shortly before MER**. Fleisig's original kinetics paper reported 64 Nm of elbow varus torque at that instant in skilled adults ([Fleisig et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1177/036354659502300218)), and a youth systematic review confirms that peak valgus torque comes just before MER ([Thompson et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738117738189)). The quantity is a *net* joint moment. It includes muscle and bony contributions, so it is not the load on the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) ([PMC8130712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8130712/)).

### The scale problem: lab torque is roughly double sleeve torque

Motion capture puts peak torque at **about 50 Nm for high school and 86 Nm for professionals** during arm cocking ([Luera et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967118760780)). A recent high-school study at 480 Hz reported 53, 59 and 69 Nm at 50%, 75% and 100% effort ([Hyeamang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671251374296)). Youth values cluster at 18–37 Nm ([Thompson et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738117738189)). The Motus sleeve reads far lower: **45.6 Nm for fastballs in high-school and college pitchers** ([Makhni et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2017.09.045)), and 31.9–37.1 Nm for long toss at 90–180 ft ([Lizzio et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7401161/)). WYNG's 36–42 Nm placeholder therefore sits on the **Motus/PULSE scale, not the motion-capture scale**. Users who compare against PULSE will expect 30–50 Nm, and users who compare against a lab report will expect 60–100 Nm.

The validation record shows why neither scale can simply be borrowed. Against motion capture, Motus torque correlated only moderately (**r = 0.667**). It underread by **41 Nm, or 38.7%** (106 vs 65 Nm), and the fastball-only correlation was not significant ([Boddy et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6365)). The independent Mayo study found a 9.4 ± 12.0 Nm underestimate and called the device "not accurate or valid" for arm stress ([Camp et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465211029017)). A markerless comparison found 63 vs 99 Nm ([Picado thesis](https://appstate.figshare.com/articles/thesis/DOES_HIP-SHOULDER_SEPARATION_ELBOW_VARUS_TORQUE_AND_FUNCTIONAL_MOVEMENT_RELATE_TO_PITCH_PERFORMANCE_IN_COLLEGIATE_BASEBALL_PITCHERS_/30333592)). The frequently cited 2017 "validation" was not designed as one; a co-author has acknowledged this publicly ([IJSPT letter 2024](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/116585-letter-to-the-editor-concerning-an-interval-throwing-program-for-baseball-pitchers-based-upon-workload-data)). A fabric sleeve showed the same pattern against KinaTrax. Its test–retest reliability was excellent (ICC 0.96), but its **limits of agreement ran from −26 to +40 Nm**, and the authors concluded it "does not measure the same construct" ([Wolf et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-0547.25)). Every published wearable shows the same profile: reliable within an athlete, moderately correlated with the lab, and wrong in absolute terms. Motion capture is not a perfect reference either, because the choice of joint-centre model alone spreads published values across 45–120 Nm ([PMC8130712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8130712/)).

### Proposal: two-segment inverse dynamics, kinematics only

WYNG's sensor set allows something a single forearm sensor cannot do. The upper-arm and forearm pucks together give elbow flexion, forearm pronation and a better elbow-joint-centre estimate. The proposed method treats the forearm, hand and ball as rigid bodies. It uses de Leva's parameters, scaled to body mass and forearm length: forearm **1.62% of body mass, centre of mass at 45.74% of forearm length from the elbow**; hand 0.61% of body mass, centre of mass at 79.0% of hand length from the wrist (male values; [de Leva 1996](https://ebm.ufabc.edu.br/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/Leva-1996.pdf); [Visual3D table](https://wiki.has-motion.com/Adjusted_Zatsiorsky-Seluyanov%27s_segment_inertia_parameters)). The ball is a 0.142 kg point mass held at the hand until release ([Moore et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465231213333)). Driveline's own motion-capture kinetics used de Leva parameters ([Boddy et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6365)). For each sample, the method:

1. derives angular velocity and acceleration from the forearm gyro;
2. derives each centre-of-mass acceleration from the accelerometer, using the rigid-body transfer terms α×r and ω×(ω×r);
3. forms the elbow moment M_E = Σ[I·α + ω×(I·ω)] + Σ r_{E→c} × m(a_c − g);
4. takes the component about the elbow's anterior–posterior (varus–valgus) axis.

Arm load is the maximum of that component between estimated foot contact and release. The research notes stress one sensitivity in particular: the ball and hand sit 0.3–0.4 m from the elbow, so **each 1 cm of moment-arm error shifts torque by roughly 3%**. Per-donning calibration of the IMU-to-elbow offset, which the ultrasonic distances can support, is therefore worth more than any refinement of the segment table. The low-pass cut-off applied before differentiating ω also moves the peak. WYNG should fix one value (the notes suggest somewhere in 20–50 Hz) and version-lock it.

Keep EMG out of the torque number. Net varus torque needs only kinematics and inertia, and a Europe PMC search found **no study that uses surface EMG to estimate pitching varus torque**. The forearm flexor-pronator channel is better used as a separate, exploratory signal, for example activation per unit torque across a session. Muscle-level work shows that the flexor-pronator mass shields the UCL, but phase-averaged EMG data are "not accurate enough" to quantify that shielding ([PMC8130712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8130712/)). Never draw the 30–35 Nm cadaver UCL failure value as a risk line. Pitchers exceed it on almost every throw, yet most never tear the ligament ([PMC8130712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8130712/)). The evidence that torque predicts injury is mixed. In 23 professionals, valgus torque at MER was associated with elbow injury ([Anz et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546510363402)). In 202 youth pitchers, baseline torque did not predict injury, while age and pitch count did ([Saito et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/arj.70068)).

**Which scale to report.** The physics-based method will naturally produce numbers on the lab scale, because it models the same mechanics as motion-capture inverse dynamics. It will probably not match the lab exactly, since soft-tissue artefact and filtering differ. The recommendation is to report **WYNG's native inverse-dynamics value, labelled "estimated elbow varus torque (Nm)"**. Present it chiefly relative to the athlete's own baseline: velocity predicts torque well within a pitcher but not between pitchers ([Luera et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967118760780)). Replace the 36–42 Nm placeholder with level-appropriate lab ranges only after a first motion-capture comparison confirms WYNG's offset. If the product owner prefers PULSE-comparable numbers, that requires an explicit, documented scaling factor, and it inherits PULSE's proportional bias. Confidence is high on the definition and method, and low on absolute accuracy until WYNG runs a simultaneous motion-capture study reporting bias, limits of agreement, proportional bias and within-athlete correlation. Youth athletes need a lower-confidence flag, because scaled adult tables mis-estimate children's segment masses ([Sterner et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/14763141.2020.1715470)).

## Release and MER are detectable from the arm, but foot plant must be estimated

The delivery's timing is remarkably stable. **Foot contact to release takes about 145–160 ms**, and **MER to release only about 20–30 ms**. In adolescents, foot contact to release was 0.15 ± 0.04 s, foot contact to MER 0.12 ± 0.04 s, and MER to release 0.02 ± 0.01 s ([mound vs flat-ground study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259743764_A_Biomechanical_Comparison_of_Pitching_From_a_Mound_Versus_Flat_Ground_in_Adolescent_Baseball_Pitchers)). Fleisig's kinetics work places MER 30 ms before release ([Fleisig et al. 1995](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/15419907_Kinetics_of_Baseball_Pitching_with_Implications_About_Injury_Mechanisms)). Across 231 pitchers from youth to professional, none of the six temporal parameters differed by level, while kinetics did ([Fleisig et al. 1999](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192909900127X)). That stability is what makes arm-only timing workable.

| Event (window t = 0 at release) | Nominal | Prior range | Detection approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foot contact | −150 ms | −130 to −180 ms | Estimated offset, ±40 ms; label "estimated" |
| MER | −30 ms | −20 to −50 ms | Sign change of humeral long-axis angular velocity |
| Release | 0 | — | Forearm \|a\| peak, refined by post-release jerk drop |

**Release.** No peer-reviewed study reports the millisecond error of IMU-detected release against high-speed video in baseball. Practice converges on the acceleration peak. A wrist-sensor patent flags release at that peak ([US 11977095](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11977095)). Mocap studies themselves only proxy release, for example as "0.01 s after the wrist passes the elbow" ([Manzi et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10601404/)). WYNG should take the forearm resultant-acceleration peak, which is already the window centre. It should refine that point with the sharp drop in |a| when the hand–ball load disappears, and cross-check it against the forearm angular-velocity peak and the elbow-extension rate between pucks. Mocap is a biased reference for this, because filtered optical data lag and flatten the hand angular-velocity peak relative to a wide-range IMU ([Lapinski et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/s19173637)). At 1920 Hz the sample period is 0.52 ms, so accuracy will be limited by how sharply the event is defined, not by the sample rate. The notes estimate ±5–10 ms, but that figure is unmeasured.

**MER.** Detect MER as the moment the upper-arm long-axis angular velocity reverses from external to internal rotation. Use it as a constraint: release must follow MER by 20–50 ms. Peak varus torque, by definition, falls just before it.

**Foot plant.** The search found **no published method that detects stride-foot contact from arm-worn sensors**. Foot-contact IMU work uses foot or pelvis sensors, which reach median errors under 20 ms against force plates ([PMC12899504](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12899504/)). WYNG should compute FC_est = release − 150 ms (or MER − 120 ms), clamp it to physiological bounds, and label it "estimated foot plant (arm-derived)" with a ±40 ms uncertainty matching the published SD. The notes raise two plausible refinements, a lead-leg impact transient in the upper-arm accelerometers and the onset of rapid humeral external rotation. Both are untested hypotheses. Until FC_est is validated against video, it should only bound the arm-load search window, never feed a displayed timing metric such as "foot plant to MER". Confidence is medium–high on the timing priors and low on detection accuracy, which WYNG must measure itself against ≥1000 fps video.

## Daily CBR recovery fits a 1–1.5 day decaying fatigue state, not a ratio

The case against ACWR is strong. Nesting the acute window inside the chronic one creates a spurious correlation, so the same data give an ACWR of 1.45 coupled and 1.71 uncoupled ([Lolli et al. 2019](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/15/921)). ACWR's relationship to injury is neither consistent nor causal ([Impellizzeri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)), and the same group now argues for dismissing the underlying theory altogether ([Impellizzeri et al. 2021](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf)). The problem is the ratio, not exponential smoothing. An EWMA with λ = 2/(N+1) ([Williams et al. 2017](https://typeset.io/pdf/better-way-to-determine-the-acute-chronic-workload-ratio-21wkg6dbsr.pdf)) is simply a discrete exponential decay with τ ≈ −1/ln(1−λ) days: N = 7 gives τ ≈ 3.5 d, and N = 3 gives τ ≈ 1.4 d. That makes the Banister fitness–fatigue model the natural structure. Its parameters, however, are poorly identifiable and depend on how load is measured. The authors of one methodological review advise against generic constants and recommend "data-informed" use instead ([Vermeire et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00426-x)). The familiar 42 d / 7 d defaults come from training platforms, not from peer-reviewed estimates, and no throwing-specific constants exist.

The arm-specific time constant comes instead from recovery studies. Shoulder rotation range of motion remained reduced at 24 h in 67 professionals ([PubMed 17991783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17991783)). In youth, all shoulder changes had recovered by 72 h ([Hirosaki Med J](https://hirosaki.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/6738/files/Hirosaki%20Med%20J%2072-1-4_24.pdf)). In adolescents, external-rotation strength recovered by day 5 ([J Athl Train](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/aop/article-10.4085-1062-6050-0052.25/article-10.4085-1062-6050-0052.25.xml)). In starters, resting HRV returned to baseline by about 48 h ([UWM](https://uwm.edu/news/?p=53401)). Together these point to a **dominant arm-fatigue τ of about 1–1.5 days**, with a minor slow tail. The usable public dose-to-rest mapping is MLB Pitch Smart. For ages 19–22, it requires 0 days of rest for 1–30 pitches, then 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 days for 31–45, 46–60, 61–80, 81–105 and 106+ pitches ([MLB Pitch Smart 19–22](https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines/ages-19-22)). Ages 9–12 use 20-pitch-wide bands up to 4 days ([MLB Pitch Smart 9–12](https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines/ages-9-12)).

**Proposal.** Let D(s) be ΣSU on day s, counting every throw: pitch counts alone miss 10–42% of throws ([Dowling et al.](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/attr/55/9/article-p911.pdf)).

1. Fatigue: F(d) = Σ_{s<d} D(s)·e^{−(d−s)/τF}, with τF ≈ 1–1.5 d. Optionally add a slow component with τ ≈ 4 d and weight about 0.3.
2. Tolerance: T(d) is an *uncoupled* decay of D with τ ≈ 28–42 d, lagged so that it excludes the fatigue window.
3. Capacity: C(d) = C_min + (C_max − C_min)·(1 − e^{−T/T_ref}).
4. Charge = clamp(100·(1 − F/C), 0, 100).

This keeps any chronic term out of a ratio and uses it only as a bounded capacity scaler. A single exponential grows only logarithmically with dose, while Pitch Smart rest rises roughly linearly. Calibrate τF, the dose exponent and the second compartment per age band, so that an outing at each band's upper limit returns Charge above the "ready" line after exactly the table's rest days. This anchors CBR to a public guideline without claiming to predict injury.

**Optional inputs.** Apply each optional input as a multiplier: DailyCBR = clamp(Charge·Π mᵢ, 0, 100). When an input is missing, mᵢ = 1. When present, mᵢ = 1 + βᵢ·clip(zᵢ, −2, 2), where zᵢ is scored against the athlete's own 14–28 day baseline, the same pattern Oura uses ([Oura support](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360057791533)). Activate an input only after 7 valid baseline days. Local arm soreness should carry the largest weight (β ≈ 0.10), with HRV and sleep at about 0.05 each. No study links whole-body HRV or sleep to local shoulder or elbow recovery. An alternative is to apply the modifiers to τF instead of to the score, which keeps the default recovery rate whenever data are missing. Cold-start users get population defaults, never a penalty. Confidence is high on the structure and on avoiding ratios, medium on τF, and low on the modifier weights, which remain a product decision until WYNG has velocity or execution-drift data for weak per-athlete calibration. The score should be framed as a training-readiness guide, because no peer-reviewed non-ratio readiness model for throwers exists to validate against.

## The pitch classifier needs F cutoffs, shrinkage and real likelihoods

The research team ran its own Monte Carlo simulations, using Gaussian data with p = 4 and n = 10–30 throws per pitch type. They expose three defects in the current specification. The results are directional, because real pitch features are not Gaussian.

**Covariance shrinkage.** Even at n = 30, the sample covariance carries about 30% relative error. Shrinkage helps most at n = 10, where Ledoit–Wolf cuts the error from 0.547 to 0.498. **z-score the four features** with athlete-wide means and SDs. This is required, because both Ledoit–Wolf ([Ledoit & Wolf 2004](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jmvana:v:88:y:2004:i:2:p:365-411)) and OAS ([Chen et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.4698)) shrink toward a scaled identity, which is meaningless for features in different units. Then apply OAS: ρ = min(1, [(1−2/p)·tr(S²) + tr(S)²] / [(n+1−2/p)·(tr(S²) − tr(S)²/p)]). The 2/p terms must stay in. scikit-learn drops them as negligible for large p ([scikit-learn source](https://github.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/blob/main/sklearn/covariance/_shrunk_covariance.py)), but at p = 4, 2/p = 0.5, so a Swift port should not copy that simplification. Floor ρ at 0.1 below n = 15. Then pool partially, following Friedman's regularized discriminant analysis ([klaR rda docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/klaR/html/rda.html)): Σ_k = (1−η)·Σ_k^shrunk + η·Σ_pooled. Use η ≈ 0.5 below n = 15, tapering to about 0.2 by n = 30. (η is used here to avoid confusion with the Consistency λ.) In simulation, this blend was never the worst variant and was close to the best in every setting. Unregularized per-type covariance at n = 10 was dangerously overconfident: throws it rated P ≥ 0.85 were only 86–88% correct. A 4×4 matrix fits in `simd_double4x4`, so the whole method is about a day of work, including tests against scikit-learn. Robust minimum covariance determinant estimation is not viable at these sample sizes; a single leave-one-out outlier pass is enough.

**Distance cutoffs.** When μ and Σ are estimated from n throws, a new throw's D² is not χ²(4). It follows **D² ~ [p(n+1)(n−1)/(n(n−p))]·F(p, n−p)**, the p-dimensional form of the prediction ellipse documented by SAS ([SAS docs](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/sgug/59902/HTML/default/fit_sect40.htm)). The simulation confirmed this, with 4.8% and 1.1% exceedances at the nominal 5% and 1%.

| Throws in cluster (n) | 99% cutoff on D (unshrunk S) | False-reject rate if χ² 95% (D = 3.08) is used |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 7.77 | 32.9% |
| 15 | 5.55 | 21.0% |
| 20 | 4.88 | 15.9% |
| 30 | 4.37 | 11.6% |

Shrinkage pulls D² down, so the F cutoff becomes conservative once shrinkage is applied, while χ² becomes roughly right only near n = 30. **Adopt per-cluster cutoffs**: the 99th percentile of each athlete's own leave-one-session-out D², floored at χ²₀.₉₉(4) = 13.28 and capped at the F value for that n. Never calibrate on training-set distances. They are bounded above by (n−1)²/n, which is 8.1 at n = 10, and they systematically understate new-throw distances. The 99% level suits the cost asymmetry: a false "General Volumetric" still counts toward workload, but a false assignment pollutes per-pitch metrics.

**Probabilities.** Replace softmax(−D_M) with Gaussian log-likelihood logits, ℓ_k = −½D²_k − ½log|Σ_k| + log π_k, with uniform priors at first. In simulation, softmax(−D) was strongly underconfident: only 27–49% of throws reached P ≥ 0.85, although 94–99% of those were correct. The 0.85 gate was therefore discarding most good throws. With regularized Gaussian logits, about 70% of throws pass the gate at 94–97% precision. Once enough held-out data exist, fit a single global temperature T by minimizing NLL on leave-one-session-out predictions ([Guo et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599v1)). Keep both gates, because they catch different failures. The probability gate catches throws that are ambiguous between two known types. The distance gate catches novel throws: a softmax stays confident for a throw far from every cluster if it is slightly closer to one. That is the open-space risk formalized by Scheirer et al. ([TPAMI 2013](https://www.scilit.net/publications/a4cefa1ee1a70b4d5993e767f8988f67)), and minimum Mahalanobis distance beats softmax confidence for this purpose ([Lee et al. 2018](https://alinlab.kaist.ac.kr/resource/NIPS_2018_Mahalanobis.pdf)). Log the two reject reasons separately, since they need different fixes.

**The λ choice.** A fixed λ in 100·exp(−λ·D_M) penalizes athletes with few training throws: the median genuine D is 2.49 at n = 10 versus 1.83 asymptotically. The exponential form also makes 100 unreachable in practice. **Adopt Consistency = 100·(1 − F_ref(D²))**, where F_ref is the scaled-F CDF for the cluster's n, or the empirical leave-one-session-out CDF. A median throw then scores 50, the best throws approach 100, and the score reads as "the share of your good throws that were further from centre." If the product owner keeps the exponential, set λ per cluster as ln2/D_med(n): **0.278, 0.315, 0.332 and 0.348 for n = 10, 15, 20, 30**. Using the χ² median gives λ = 0.378, but that is correct only for large n. Compute Consistency only against the assigned cluster, and suppress it for General Volumetric throws.

**Validation.** Use leave-one-session-out cross-validation, never random throw-wise folds. Record-wise splits in wearable studies inflate accuracy, for example from 85% to 99.85% in one study ([Saeb et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5441397/)). Report precision against coverage across the P threshold, the false-reject rate of the distance cutoff, a leave-one-class-out novelty test, and Wilson intervals, which run roughly ±15–20 points per athlete at n ≈ 20. Confidence is high on the structure, F cutoffs and likelihood form, and medium on η, the shrinkage floor and acceptance rates, which need WYNG's real feature distributions.

## Sensor saturation threatens every release-instant metric

The reported angular rates exceed WYNG's ±4000 dps gyro range. A Sensors paper cites forearm rates of **6000–9000 deg/s** and abandoned its ±2000 dps gyro for that reason ([Sensors 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/22/9008)). An IMU pilot reports shoulder internal-rotation velocity near **12,000 deg/s** ([Harvard Ortho Journal](https://orthojournalhms.org/volume8/manuscripts/ms02.htm)). Motion capture gives lower figures: about 4,700 deg/s for shoulder internal rotation and 2,400 deg/s for the forearm ([Boddy et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6365)). The difference is consistent with optical filtering flattening peaks ([Lapinski et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/s19173637)). Either way, the upper-arm long axis likely clips for competitive adults, and the forearm plausibly does too. The accelerometer risk is clearer: **collegiate forearm peaks reach 1238–1350 m/s², or 126–138 g, and upper-arm peaks 105–110 g** ([PMC9655623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655623/)). A ±16 g or ±32 g part would turn the acceleration peak, which is WYNG's window centre and release marker, into a flat plateau. Lapinski's team built a dedicated ±200 g node for exactly this problem ([Lapinski et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/s19173637)).

Clipping does not degrade a single metric. It corrupts the integrated orientation behind arm slot, the angular acceleration behind arm load, and the peak behind release timing, all at the same instant. Silent clipping would also cap torque, which is one plausible cause of the "accuracy falls as torque rises" pattern seen in sleeves ([IJSPT letter 2024](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/116585-letter-to-the-editor-concerning-an-interval-throwing-program-for-baseball-pitchers-based-upon-workload-data)). WYNG should confirm the accelerometer full-scale range with firmware, add a per-axis saturation flag to every sample, and withhold or downgrade any metric whose computation crosses a clipped span. Before launch, it should log the clip rate by athlete level. Confidence is medium, because the angular-rate figures vary widely between sources.

## Conclusion

The literature does not supply a ready-made arm-only pitching model. It does supply constraints tight enough that WYNG's remaining choices are mostly product decisions, not research questions. The decisions with the most leverage are the scale and wording of arm load, the cutoffs and labels for arm slot, and whether Consistency becomes a percentile. Each is cheap to decide now and expensive to change once athletes have months of history. The single largest technical risk is saturation, not algorithm choice. It deserves a firmware conversation before any metric is tuned.

WYNG's two-segment sensor layout is a real differentiator. Every validated competitor is a single forearm IMU that cannot locate the elbow or measure elbow flexion. That advantage only becomes a claim with WYNG's own simultaneous motion-capture and high-speed-video study, and the same capture session can settle release accuracy, foot-plant offsets, arm-slot bias and torque agreement together. Planning that one study is the most efficient next step toward a defensible "validated" label.
